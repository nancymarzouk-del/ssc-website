# The Simply Sexy Cigars Editorial Style Guide

*The governing document for The Journal, homepage editorial, the seasonal Editions, and every brand story.*

**Version 1.0 — Approved and Frozen, 26 July 2026.** The permanent editorial standard. Authoritative from this point forward; evaluate all editorial against it. Do not revise unless a Version 2 is explicitly requested. Companion instruments: `EDITORIAL-REVIEW-RUBRIC.md` (the pre-publication review) and `EDITORIAL-PRODUCTION-CHECKLIST.md` (imagery and technical readiness).

---

## The Creed

**We do not write content. We write literature in service of hospitality.**

Every piece exists to make a reader feel the first cool evening of autumn and quietly wish they had nowhere else to be. If a sentence does not serve that feeling, it does not belong to us.

The reference text for this voice is **`autumn-is-cigar-season.html`**. When in doubt, read it aloud and match its temperature.

---

## What This Governs

This guide applies to all editorial writing published under the Simply Sexy Cigars name:

- **The Journal** — feature articles.
- **The seasonal Editions** — Autumn, Winter, Spring, Summer.
- **Homepage editorial** — any prose that sets tone rather than sells.
- **Brand stories** — anything told in the house voice.

It does *not* govern functional copy (booking labels, confirmations, legal). That copy has its own job — *effortless* — and follows the same restraint, but not the literary standard below.

## The Voice

**Who is speaking.** A single, unhurried host — the house itself. Warm, certain, never eager. The voice of someone who has hosted a thousand evenings and has nothing left to prove.

**Person.**
- **Second person — "you"** is the home key. The reader is never a spectator of someone else's evening; they are *in the chair.* ("You feel it before you see it.")
- **First-person plural — "we"** is the house. Used **rarely, and only at the edges** — never in the middle of a story. It may appear once, near the close, to quietly own the feeling. It never names a service.
- **Third-person named characters** (a groom, a father) are permitted but costly: they turn the reader into a witness. Prefer the universal *you.*

**Tense.** Present tense wherever possible, for immediacy. The evening is happening now.

**Temperature.** Calm, confident, restrained. Warmth without eagerness. Certainty without volume.

**Diction.**
- *Yes:* concrete, sensory, timeless, plainspoken-but-elegant. Short Anglo-Saxon words carrying large feelings.
- *No:* marketing adjectives (*luxurious, exquisite, unforgettable, world-class, premier*), clinical or technical terms, modern jargon, clichés, anything that labels a feeling instead of producing it.

## The Ten Principles

*Proven on the flagship. Every article is measured against these.*

**1. Restraint over abundance.** Never add words to sound literary or to fill length. Make every sentence *inevitable* — as though there were never a better way to say it. Luxury is restraint. When a piece improves, it usually gets *shorter.*

**2. Sense of place, immediately.** The first paragraph transports the reader into the season before it explains anything. Open *inside* the scene, never above it.

**3. All five senses — observed, not decorative.** Smell, sound, taste, touch, and light must all be present across a piece (a cigar-and-harvest story a reader cannot *smell* is unfinished). Each sensory detail must deepen atmosphere, never ornament it. One well-placed detail beats three pretty ones.

**4. Observed, not invented.** The writing should feel *remembered,* not composed. Replace anything generic with lived experience. Show, never tell — *"the cork eases free with a low sigh,"* not *"a satisfying pull."*

**5. Universality through emotion, never lists.** The reader must picture *their own* celebration. Naming occasions (a wedding, a golf outing, a car delivery) narrows — every reader outside the list steps out of the story. Include everyone through the *shared feeling* beneath all occasions, not through enumeration.

**6. Rhythm.** Read every piece aloud. Vary sentence length deliberately; let short sentences create stillness; let paragraphs breathe. **Beware the rule-of-three tic** — a page where every paragraph resolves in a triad flattens. Keep the triads that earn their place; break the rest into pairs and singles.

**7. The brand almost disappears.** The reader falls in love with the *feeling* first, and only then realizes who creates it. The story is sacred; the house appears only at the edges. The brand name may leave the prose entirely and live in the signature alone. Never a service, never a price, never a pitch inside a story.

**8. Timelessness.** Assume every piece runs again each autumn for ten years. No dates, no trends, no current references, no hemisphere-specific assumptions. It should read as if written yesterday or twenty years from now.

**9. The four-title test.** Ask of every sentence: *Would this survive unchanged in Robb Report, Four Seasons Magazine, Cigar Aficionado, or Departures?* If not, rewrite it.

**10. The ending lingers.** The final paragraph leaves the reader sitting still — not because it is dramatic, but because it feels *true.* End on feeling, not on thesis. The last line should linger the way the evening lingers.

## What the Writing Is Really About

The article is **never about cigars.** The cigar is the invitation, not the subject. Every piece quietly reinforces the same seven themes:

> **slowing down · gathering · hospitality · memory · ritual · lingering · meaningful celebration**

If a paragraph does not serve at least one of these, question why it exists.

## The Two-Lens Rule

Every editorial decision serves **two goals at once**:

1. Create the feeling of reading a luxury publication.
2. Lead the reader to imagine Simply Sexy Cigars at *their own* celebration.

These are one mechanism, not two: a reader who feels they are reading something beautiful is a reader picturing themselves inside it. The moment the writing *sells,* both collapse.

**The brand as bookends.** Presence is permitted at the *entrance* (an editor's voice) and the *exit* (a single, quiet acknowledgment or invitation). The **middle stays sacred** — pure story, no brand.

**The two-goal test.** For any element: *Does it deepen the reading?* **and** *Does it help the reader picture their own celebration?* The best elements clear both. An element that clears only the second is advertising — cut it. One that clears only the first is lovely but idle.

## Structure & Craft Conventions

**Article anatomy** (the proven shape):
1. **Hero image** — full-bleed, atmospheric.
2. **Masthead** — eyebrow (the register, e.g. *The Season*) · title (with the key word in italic) · one-line subtitle · meta (*Season Year · X Minute Read*).
3. **Opening** — a lead paragraph carrying the first sensory transport, set with a drop cap.
4. **Chaptered body** — short titled chapters, alternating with full-bleed photography.
5. **One pull quote** — never more.
6. **The Moment** — the signature block.
7. **Close** — a short, lingering coda.

**The Moment block.** A restrained "brief" of the occasion, always closing a feature. Three to four rows: **Occasion** and **Setting** always; one or two contextual rows (*Pairs Beautifully With, Felt Most By*); and a final **Lesson** that states a theme as quiet truth, never a moral. It doubles, invisibly, as inspiration a reader could hand back to us.

**Pull quote.** One per article. A present-tense aphorism, usually two lines, carrying a core theme. Quotable, never clever for its own sake.

**Chapter titles.** Short. Set in the display serif with the closing (or key) word in italic and the champagne accent — *The Ritual of the* Hour.

**Drop cap.** The opening paragraph only.

**Reading measure & length.** Body copy stays near a 65-character measure. Features run **5–7 minutes.** Scarcity is the standard — a reader should be able to *finish.* Never pad.

**Headlines.** Evocative, restrained, timeless. The title may become a perennial banner (*Autumn Is Cigar Season*). No colons-as-SEO, no keyword stuffing.

**Captions.** A short, lowercase-feeling line beneath each image — a whispered note, not a description.

## Imagery & Art Direction Standard

Every image — real or placeholder — carries a full brief so photography (professional or generated) drops in without changing the layout. Each brief specifies:

> **Subject · Mood · Time of Day · Lighting · Composition · Lens · Color Palette · Textures · Atmosphere · Editorial Inspiration**

Rules:
- The photographic world is warm, cinematic, low-lit, and human — golden hour into blue hour, candlelight and firelight, real occasions.
- **No logos on photography.** The brand is the feeling in the frame, never a watermark.
- Placeholder frames must share the exact aspect ratio of the image that will replace them.
- Inspiration references stay within the four titles: Robb Report, Four Seasons Magazine, Cigar Aficionado, Departures (and Kinfolk for restraint).

## Terminology & House Style

- **Edition**, never "Issue" or "Collection." The seasonal publication is *The Autumn Edition.*
- **Named seasons, never volume numbers.** No "Vol. 1, No. 3." The year stays quiet (in the meta line, not the title).
- **The Journal** (capitalized). **The Moment** (capitalized, the signature block).
- **The tagline** — *Experience first. Cigars always.* — appears in the signature, never as ad copy.
- **Footer year** in roman numerals (*© MMXXVI*) — a small, timeless grace note.
- **Em dashes**, spaced ( — ), for the house's unhurried asides.
- **American English.** No emoji. **No exclamation points.** No ALL-CAPS for emphasis (small-caps eyebrows excepted).

## The Kill List

None of the following ever appears in editorial:

- Prices, packages, or rate language.
- "Book now" or any call-to-action inside a story (the one permitted invitation lives only at a feature's edge).
- Share buttons, comment sections, tag clouds, category filters, archives, pagination, "popular posts."
- Countdown timers or urgency/scarcity *of the service* ("limited dates!").
- Volume numbers, author avatars, author bios.
- Testimonials dressed as features (a single testimonial belongs to the Experiences pages, not The Journal).
- Superlatives and marketing adjectives; clichés; emoji; exclamation points.
- Lists that narrow the audience.

## The Voice Test

Run before any piece is approved. If any answer is *no,* refine — without adding unnecessary prose.

- [ ] Does it create a sense of place *immediately*?
- [ ] Can the reader smell it, hear it, taste it, feel the temperature, see the light?
- [ ] Does it feel *observed*, not invented?
- [ ] Does the reader picture *their own* celebration, not someone else's?
- [ ] Does it read aloud with varied, breathing rhythm?
- [ ] Does the brand almost disappear — feeling first, recognition second?
- [ ] Could it be republished, unchanged, every autumn for ten years?
- [ ] Would every sentence survive in Robb Report, Four Seasons, Cigar Aficionado, or Departures?
- [ ] Does the ending leave the reader sitting still because it feels *true*?
- [ ] Is it *shorter* than it wants to be?

## The Voice in Practice

Four refinements from the flagship edit, as teaching examples:

**Broaden through emotion, not lists.**
> *Before:* "…the milestone birthday, the keys to something long promised, the friends on the clubhouse veranda long after the round is scored."
> *After:* "Different rooms, different reasons, and beneath every one the same wish: to make the night last."

**Show, don't tell.**
> *Before:* "the cork gives with a soft, satisfying pull."
> *After:* "the cork eases free with a low sigh."

**Let the brand disappear.**
> *Before:* "It is the only thing we have ever set out to make, at Simply Sexy Cigars: not the loudest evening, but the one that lingers."
> *After:* "That hour — the one that stays with you long after everything else has ended — is the only thing we have ever set out to make." *(The name now lives only in the signature.)*

**End on feeling, not thesis.**
> *Before:* "Autumn is cigar season. It always has been."
> *After:* "Let the evening keep you. You have nowhere else to be."
